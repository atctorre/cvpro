How to write a delivery driver resume that builds trust

Restaurants, pharmacies, couriers and logistics companies hire drivers in volume, but what they fear most is the one who crashes, loses a package or disappears. The resume that gets hired does not brag about speed. It proves a valid license, a clean record and route knowledge. In this trade, trust outweighs velocity.

What hiring managers check on a driver resume

  • License type and status. Class, expiration, and whether you have a clean MVR. It is the first thing checked; if it is missing, the reading stops.
  • Driving record. No at-fault accidents, no major violations, no DOT issues. If you can honestly say it, say it — it is the most reassuring line you can write.
  • Route and area knowledge. Neighborhoods, zip codes, traffic patterns. A driver who knows the area completes more stops per hour with fewer failed deliveries.
  • Handling packages, cash and devices. COD collections, end-of-shift reconciliation, scanner and route apps. Show you are careful with what is not yours.
  • Customer interaction. You are the face of the business at the customer's door. One complaint gets remembered; so does one good review.

Mistakes that get a driver resume rejected

  • Leaving license details off. No class, no status, no expiration — the manager assumes there is a problem. Put it in the first section.
  • Not stating vehicle situation. Many roles require your own vehicle; others provide one. Clarify to avoid a wasted call.
  • Bragging about speed. "Fastest driver on the team" reads as accident risk. Better: "met delivery windows with a clean safety record".
  • Skipping cash or COD handling. If you collected payments, that is trust-based experience. Include it with the reconciliation.
  • An out-of-date phone number. Obvious, but this field hires by phone the same day. A number that does not answer is a candidate who does not exist.

If you have never worked as a delivery driver

What matters is that you drive responsibly and know the area. List your license class and status, how many years you have been driving, and any informal delivery, errands or driving for a family business — all of it counts. Jobs that required punctuality and handling money (cashier, sales, collections) show reliability; include them. A short defensive-driving course sets you apart and often lowers the employer's insurance concern.

Frequently asked questions

Should I mention a minor accident?

Not on the resume — it is not the place. If asked in the interview, answer honestly and explain what changed. Lying in the interview is what disqualifies you, especially since the MVR will show it.

Do I need my own vehicle?

Depends on the employer. If you have one, list year, model and insurance status. If not, state you are available for roles that provide a vehicle.

Should I list gig-app delivery work?

Yes. Name the platform, the period, approximate deliveries per day and your rating if it was strong. It is real, measurable experience.

Do I need a high school diploma?

Usually not. List whatever education you have honestly. The license and driving record carry the weight.

How long should the resume be?

One page, with license, phone and availability readable in the first five seconds. Everything else is supporting detail.
