How to write a hair stylist resume that proves technique and clientele

Salons hire on two things that rarely make it onto a resume: the techniques you actually own and whether you bring clients with you. A salon owner knows a stylist with an established book generates revenue in week one. Your resume must state exactly what you can do — and with which product lines — instead of generic "cuts and color".

What the salon owner or manager looks for

  • Specific techniques. Balayage, babylights, foiling, color correction, keratin and smoothing treatments, razor and scissor cutting, updos and event styling, extensions. The generic list says nothing; the specific one does.
  • Product lines you know. Redken, Wella, Schwarzkopf, L'Oréal Professionnel, Olaplex, Aveda. The salon already carries a line and wants to know if training is needed.
  • Client book. If you have clients who follow you, say so honestly: "established clientele of repeat guests". It is the strongest point in any negotiation.
  • License and continuing education. State cosmetology license with number and expiration, brand classes, advanced academy training. Recent education outweighs years in this trade.
  • Portfolio. Link to a professional Instagram or portfolio with real work. Non-negotiable: stylists are hired on what can be seen.

Mistakes that get a stylist resume rejected

  • "Cutting, styling and color" with no specifics. Every stylist writes it. Detail the techniques you perform independently and at what level.
  • No portfolio or social link. In this trade, a resume without images is incomplete. Link your work account, not your personal one.
  • Omitting the client book. It is your largest commercial asset. Mention it without inventing numbers.
  • Not mentioning retail sales and booking. A stylist who books efficiently and sells retail at the chair adds margin. Owners value it highly.
  • A sloppy resume layout. You work in an aesthetic trade. A disorganized resume contradicts your own service.

If you just finished cosmetology school

Recent training and a portfolio outweigh years here. List your school and license status (or exam date if pending), and above all build a real portfolio: before-and-after photos of your own work, even from school or on friends and family, shot in good light without filters. Three well-photographed transformations beat any adjective. Add brand classes you have completed and openness to starting as an assistant — in many salons that is the standard path, shampooing and assisting while you build a book.

Frequently asked questions

How do I write a hair stylist resume with no experience?

With a portfolio. Photograph three or four of your own transformations in good light, list your school and license status, add brand classes, and state openness to an assistant role. This trade hires on what it can see.

Should I write hair stylist, cosmetologist or hairdresser?

Use the posting's term. In your summary you can bridge: "Licensed cosmetologist specializing in color and cutting".

Should I mention my client book?

Yes — it is your strongest negotiating asset. State it honestly as "established clientele" without inventing figures.

Do I need to include Instagram?

Yes, the professional account showing your work. It is the industry-standard portfolio. If you do not have one, build it before applying.

How do I present retail sales?

As a commercial result: "Consistent retail attachment on color services with repeat product purchases". Owners measure margin, not just service.
