How to write a chef resume that proves you can run service

In kitchens, the resume exists only to get you a stage or a trail shift. The executive chef reading it looks for three signals: which stations you have owned, what type of kitchen and what volume, and whether you understand food cost and waste. Everything else gets tested on the line. A resume built on "passion for food" with no station named does not even earn the trail.

What the executive chef looks for

  • Stations owned. Grill, sauté, garde manger, pastry, fry, expo. State which you ran solo and which you supported. That is how a brigade gets built.
  • Kitchen type and covers. Fine dining, hotel, banquet, catering, institutional, fast casual. Covers per service. A 300-cover night has nothing in common with a 40-cover one.
  • Food cost and waste control. Recipe costing, portion control, inventory, waste reduction, par levels. It is what separates a cook from a chef.
  • Sanitation and certification. Food handler card, ServSafe Manager, HACCP, temperature logs, FIFO. Health inspections can close a restaurant; the chef needs confidence here.
  • Menu development with judgment. If you contributed to a menu, say what you added and whether you factored cost and seasonality. Creativity without costing does not interest anyone.

Mistakes that get a chef resume rejected

  • "Passionate about food" in the summary. It says nothing and everyone writes it. Replace it with stations, covers and kitchen type.
  • Not naming stations. "Cook" could mean anything. The chef needs to know where to place you on day one.
  • Omitting service volume. Without covers per service, your experience cannot be sized.
  • Leaving out sanitation. If ServSafe or HACCP never appear, the chef assumes you do not manage it — a health risk.
  • Inflating the title. Calling yourself "chef" when you were a line cook shows up in five minutes on the line. Use the real title and show progression.

If you are starting in professional kitchens

Kitchens teach on the job, and nearly everyone starts on prep or dish. Get your food handler card before applying — it is inexpensive, fast and required in most states. If you attended culinary school, detail externships with establishment, station and covers. If you cooked in a family business or did catering on your own, list it with cuisine type and how many you served. Above all, make night, weekend and holiday availability explicit: in this trade it is the number-one filter.

Frequently asked questions

How do I write a cook resume with no experience?

Get a food handler card, detail externships or family-business cooking with station and volume, and make night and weekend availability clear. The rest is decided on the trail shift.

Should I write cook or chef?

The real title you held. "Chef" implies command of a brigade. If you were a line cook, say so and show progression: prep → line → station lead.

How do I state volume?

Covers per service or per day: "200-cover weekend service". It is the reference every chef reads instantly.

Should I mention food cost knowledge?

Yes, if genuine. Recipe costing, portion control and waste tracking are what mark a cook who can move up to sous chef.

How long should the resume be?

One page. Stations and kitchen type up top, then establishments with covers and what you owned at each.
