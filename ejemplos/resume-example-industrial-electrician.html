How to write an electrician resume that proves safety and technical competence

An electrician gets hired for two reasons and fired for one: hired for skill and safety, fired for causing an injury or a production stoppage. The maintenance manager reading your resume looks for license status, the types of systems you have worked on, and evidence you follow procedure. Skill without safety is worth nothing here.

What the maintenance manager looks for on an electrician resume

  • License and level. Journeyman or master license with state and expiration, apprenticeship completion, hours logged. First section, with dates.
  • Type of systems. Industrial (motors, MCCs, PLCs, 480V three-phase, medium voltage), commercial, residential. Low and medium voltage are different worlds; specify where you have worked.
  • Electrical safety. Lockout/tagout, NFPA 70E, arc-flash PPE, OSHA 10 or 30, energized vs de-energized work procedures. Every safety credential counts.
  • Troubleshooting and maintenance. Faults diagnosed, PM programs executed, downtime reduced. "Diagnosed and repaired production-line fault in under 2 hours" is an achievement.
  • Prints and instruments. One-line diagrams, schematics, multimeter, megohmmeter, thermal imaging, VFDs, conduit bending.

Mistakes that get an electrician resume rejected

  • License not visible. If the recruiter cannot find your license in the first section, they assume you are an unlicensed helper.
  • "Electrical installations" with no detail. Residential, commercial and industrial require different competencies. State which, with voltages and equipment.
  • Omitting safety. If LOTO, PPE and NFPA 70E never appear, the maintenance manager assumes you work without procedures. Disqualifying.
  • Not mentioning shift and on-call availability. Industrial maintenance runs 24/7. Stating you take emergency calls moves you up.
  • Leaving out self-employed work. Independent installations done to code are experience. Describe type, scope and code followed.

If you just finished your apprenticeship or trade program

Maintenance managers hire apprentices and junior electricians regularly, but they want to see training and a safety mindset. List your trade program or apprenticeship with hours, the installations you worked on with equipment types, and every safety credential (OSHA 10, LOTO, first aid). If you did residential work independently or helped a licensed electrician, describe it precisely. State that you can read prints and use test instruments — that is what separates a technician from a helper. Note your license exam status if you are eligible.

Frequently asked questions

How do I write an electrician resume with little formal experience?

Lead with your trade program or apprenticeship hours, installations by type, safety credentials, and independent work described precisely. Mention print reading and test instruments.

Should I write electrician or industrial electrician?

Use the posting's term. For plant roles, "industrial" in title and summary. For general roles, "electrician" with the areas you cover.

Is a license required?

It depends on the state and sector, but industrial employers almost always require or strongly prefer it. If you do not have it yet, it is the fastest path to a higher level.

Should I mention an accident I was involved in?

Not on the resume. If asked, answer honestly and explain what changed in how you work. Hiding it is what disqualifies if it surfaces.

How long should the resume be?

One page. License and safety at the top, then experience by system type and maintenance results.
