How to write a maintenance manager resume that proves uptime

Maintenance is measured by what does not happen: the plant did not stop, the equipment did not fail, production did not slip. That is why a maintenance manager's resume must speak in uptime, unplanned downtime and PM compliance. A resume that only lists equipment repaired describes a technician, not someone who manages plant reliability.

What the plant manager evaluates

  • Uptime and downtime. Equipment availability percentage, unplanned downtime hours, MTBF and MTTR. It is the role's central metric.
  • Preventive program. PM compliance rate, critical equipment coverage, shift from reactive to planned maintenance. It is evidence of management, not reaction.
  • Equipment and technology. Production lines, boilers, compressors, electrical systems, refrigeration, PLCs, hydraulics and pneumatics. Specify the asset base you managed.
  • Team and contractors. Technicians supervised, shifts covered, contractors coordinated. Maintenance is managing people and specialized vendors.
  • Budget and spare parts. Cost control, critical spares inventory, vendor negotiation. A missing part stops the plant.

Mistakes that get a maintenance manager resume rejected

  • Listing repairs instead of metrics. Repairing is the technical function. Managing is making repairs less necessary. Show uptime and PM compliance.
  • Not specifying the asset base. Maintaining a food plant and a textile mill involve different equipment and risks.
  • Omitting preventive work. If you only discuss corrective work, the manager assumes you fight fires rather than prevent them.
  • Not mentioning safety. Lockout/tagout, hot work permits, confined space, fall protection. Maintenance accidents are severe and frequent.
  • No spare parts management. Critical inventory control is the manager's responsibility. Omitting it leaves out half the function.

If you are a technician seeking your first supervisory role

The step up is proven through management, not technical skill. If you scheduled work orders, kept equipment history, coordinated other technicians, controlled spares or built the PM plan, write it in that language: those are supervisory functions even if your title was technician. Learn a CMMS, even a basic one — it separates you fastest from a pure technician. Add industrial safety and root cause analysis training. And prepare one example of a recurring failure you eliminated at the source, because that is the question that decides the interview.

Frequently asked questions

How do I move from technician to maintenance supervisor?

Document management functions you already performed: work order scheduling, equipment history, technician coordination, spares control, PM plan development.

Which metrics should I include?

Equipment uptime, unplanned downtime and PM compliance. If you did not track them, describe program coverage and the asset base you maintained.

How important is CMMS experience?

Very. It distinguishes organized management from reactive work. If you have not used one, learning a basic system is a worthwhile investment.

How do I show I am preventive, not reactive?

With a case: "Shifted 60% of interventions from corrective to planned in the first year through a criticality-based PM program". Concrete and verifiable.

Should I mention on-call availability?

Yes. Industrial maintenance means after-hours calls. Stating it clearly puts you ahead.
