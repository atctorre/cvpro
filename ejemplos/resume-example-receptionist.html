How to write a receptionist resume that makes the right first impression

The receptionist is the first person a client, vendor or job candidate sees. The person hiring for this role is not looking for someone to "greet visitors". They want someone who represents the company's image while controlling access, phones and calendars without letting anything slip. Your resume is the first impression of the first impression you will make.

What hiring managers look for in a receptionist

  • Phone system and volume. Calls and visitors handled per day, how you routed them, multi-line system or scheduling software used. Volume proves you can handle the pace.
  • Access control and logging. Visitor logs, badge issuance, mail and package handling, conference room management. It is the security side of the job and many leave it out.
  • Presentation and communication. Correct writing, professional phone manner, polished appearance. The resume itself is the test: no typos, clean alignment, restrained design.
  • Tools. Outlook or Google Calendar, basic Excel, industry scheduling or CRM software (medical, hospitality, legal). Name them.
  • Discretion. The front desk hears and sees everything. Showing you understand confidentiality reassures the employer.

Mistakes that get a receptionist resume rejected

  • Typos or messy formatting. Instant disqualification. If your resume is disorganized, the manager pictures your front desk the same way.
  • "Greeted visitors and answered phones" alone. Describes the job, not you. Add volume, tools and something you solved: "managed an 8-line phone system and scheduling for 5 exam rooms".
  • Not naming the industry. Medical, hotel, law firm and corporate reception are different jobs. Specify and adapt the vocabulary.
  • Vague availability. "Available immediately" is not enough. Specify: Saturdays, evening shift, rotating. In hotels and clinics it decides the hire.
  • Including a photo. US resumes do not include photos. Some employers discard those that do to avoid bias concerns.

If you have never worked a front desk

Any public-facing role is a stepping stone to reception: cashier, host, customer service, retail associate, helping at a family business. Rewrite it in front-desk vocabulary: "in-person and phone customer service", "appointment scheduling", "visitor check-in". If you write well, prove it in the resume itself. If you speak Spanish or another language, list it with your level — in hotels, medical offices and companies with diverse clients, it often decides between two similar candidates.

Frequently asked questions

Should I include a photo?

No. In the United States resumes do not carry photos, and including one can get your application set aside.

Should I list social media skills?

Only if the posting mentions it or the company is small. Corporate reception does not value it; a small business might.

How do I show I handle pressure?

With volume: calls per day, visitors, rooms managed at once. "Works well under pressure" as a phrase convinces no one; the number does.

Do I need a college degree?

Usually not. A high school diploma or GED plus customer service or office courses is standard. List any college coursework in progress.

Should I mention a receptionist job at a very small office?

Yes. A three-person office where you did everything shows versatility. Describe the actual functions, not the size.
