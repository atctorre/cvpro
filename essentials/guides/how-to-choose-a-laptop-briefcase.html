How to Choose a Laptop Briefcase: Size, Leather Terms and What to Check Before You Buy

To choose a laptop briefcase, measure the laptop first. Screen size is a diagonal and says nothing about width, depth or thickness. Add room for a charger and documents, then compare the total with the bag's interior dimensions. A listing that gives only a size label leaves the fit unconfirmed.

This guide is for anyone buying a briefcase, ours or not. Our own five items are held to the same checks near the end.

Start with the laptop, not the bag

A laptop's screen size is the diagonal of the screen. Two laptops with the same screen size can differ in width, depth and thickness, so the label is a starting point, not a measurement. Measure the laptop itself and write down three numbers.

What to measureWhere to find itWhat to compare it with
WidthSpec sheet, or a ruler across the closed lidInterior width on the product page
DepthSpec sheet, or a ruler front to backInterior depth on the product page
ThicknessSpec sheet, or a ruler at the thickest pointInterior thickness on the product page

Then add room for a charger and a few documents, and compare the total with the bag's interior dimensions, not its outside dimensions, which include the walls and lining.

Look for a stated padded sleeve. A word such as "interlayer" or "laptop compartment", with no measurements and no mention of padding, is not a promise of fit: it tells you a divider exists, not how large it is or how well it protects.

The 15-inch briefcase lists "Size: 15 inches" and a "Computer Interlayer". Neither lists interior dimensions or padding, so we tell buyers to measure their laptop and to email hola@getcvpro.com if it is close to the limit.

Briefcase, messenger bag or satchel: what changes

The three names overlap, and sellers apply them loosely. Judge a bag by how it is carried, how it is built and how it closes, and read the specification fields, not the title.

BriefcaseMessenger bagSatchel
How it is carriedBy a handle, in the handAcross the body, on a long strapBy a handle or a shoulder strap
StructureTypically firmer and flatter, holds its shapeTypically soft and roomyTypically semi-structured
ClosureTypically a zipper, a hasp or a latchTypically a flap with buckles or a magnetTypically a flap or a top zipper

In use, a leather messenger bag spreads the weight across the body and leaves your hands free, while a briefcase puts the weight in one hand and keeps papers flat. A leather satchel suits someone who wants a flap closure and a softer shape. When you shop for a mens messenger bag, check whether the strap is included and whether interior dimensions are stated.

Leather terms and what sellers have to disclose

In the United States, the FTC's Guides for the Leather Industry (16 CFR Part 24) set out how leather may be described. Four points matter when you read a listing.

  • The unqualified word "leather." It should describe a product composed in all substantial parts of leather (section 24.2).
  • Bonded or reconstituted material. It should not be represented as leather. If a seller uses "bonded leather," the percentage of leather fiber and of non-leather substances has to be disclosed (24.2(f)).
  • Imitation finishes and backing. Leather processed to imitate another type, or backed with another material, calls for disclosing the type and the backing (24.2(b) and (c)).
  • Where disclosures go. On the label and in advertising (24.2(g)).

The phrase "genuine leather" does not appear in the text of Part 24, so the FTC has not defined it. Sellers use it often, and it does not tell you the grade. Grade terms such as "full grain" need evidence, and listings often state them without a certificate. Treat a named grade as a claim to check.

Every material statement on our product pages is labeled seller-stated. Three of our five listings use the term "full grain" in the material composition field. That is the seller's wording, no certificate comes with any listing, and we have not tested the material. We will check a sample before any label changes. The rule text is at eCFR, 16 CFR Part 24.

Lining, closures, handles and pockets

Four details decide how a briefcase behaves on a normal workday.

  • Lining. The lining is what touches your laptop and papers. Both of our briefcases list "Lining Material: COTTON". A listing that does not name the lining leaves that question open.
  • Closure. A zipper closes the full opening. A hasp is a metal clasp that fastens a flap or the top edge. The 15-inch briefcase lists "Zipper & Hasp", and the 17-inch briefcase lists "Hasp".
  • Handles. A shoulder strap changes how the weight sits, but only if the listing says one is included. Both briefcases list "Handle/Strap Type: soft handle" and "Number of Handles/Straps: Single", and no shoulder strap is listed.
  • Pockets. Count the pockets you will use. Both briefcases list an interior compartment, a cell phone pocket, an interior zipper pocket and an interior slot pocket, plus an exterior pocket the seller writes as "Silt Pocket".

When a listing is silent, ask the seller before you order. Four questions cover most gaps:

  1. What are the interior dimensions?
  2. Is the laptop sleeve padded?
  3. Is a shoulder strap included, or only handles?
  4. What does the bag weigh, and in which unit? A bare "2" could be kilograms or pounds.

What to check when a bag ships from overseas

Much of the risk in buying an imported bag is in what the page leaves out. Look for four things.

  • A delivery range, stated before checkout. The FTC's Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule requires a seller to have a reasonable basis for any shipping promise, and to ask for your consent if an order will be late.
  • Who pays import costs. Duties on imported goods are sometimes collected by the carrier at delivery, so a page that does not say who pays them leaves a gap.
  • Written terms for a problem order. Look for what the seller says in writing about an order that arrives damaged, differs from the listing, runs late or never arrives.
  • Where the bag ships from. Our listings state Mainland China / CN / Guangdong as the origin. Orders are fulfilled by an overseas supplier, and we do not keep stock in the United States.

A checklist you can use on any product page

Check each of these eight items against the listing.

  1. Your laptop's measurements, compared with the interior dimensions.
  2. Padding that is stated, not implied by a word such as "interlayer".
  3. A material statement, with its source: the seller, a label or a certificate.
  4. A named lining.
  5. A named closure: zipper, hasp or both.
  6. A named handle or strap type, and whether a shoulder strap is included.
  7. A weight with a unit.
  8. A delivery range, and written terms for late, damaged or mismatched orders.

If a listing fails an item that matters to you, ask the seller. If the answer is vague, choose another bag.
