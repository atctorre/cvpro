How to Write a Resume With No Experience

Many postings say "1-2 years required," and you've never held a job with that title, or any job at all. It feels like a dead end, but it isn't.

Everyone starts with a first resume. What changes the outcome isn't years of work history — it's how clearly you present what you actually have. Here's what to include, in what order, and an example you can model yours on.

Why "no experience" resumes still work

Employers hiring for entry-level roles, internships, or first jobs expect candidates without a long work history — that's the premise of an entry-level posting. What a hiring manager screens for is whether you show up on time, follow instructions, and can be trusted with basic responsibility.

None of that requires a job title. It shows up in how you talk about a class project finished under a deadline, a volunteer shift you didn't skip, or a sibling you looked after every weekday for a year. The mistake isn't lacking experience — it's leaving the section blank instead of filling it with what you actually did.

A resume with no experience competes against other resumes with no experience, not a senior candidate's. You need to sound ready to start, not like someone who has already run a department.

What to include instead of work history

None of these need a paycheck attached to count — what matters is that you can describe them honestly and specifically.

  • Internships, paid or unpaid, even short ones — say what you did day to day.
  • Volunteering: a one-time event, a recurring shift at a shelter or food bank, or organizing something for a cause.
  • Class or capstone projects, especially with a deliverable: a report, a prototype, a presentation to a real audience.
  • Informal or family-business work, like helping at a relative's shop, restaurant, or farm, even with no formal paycheck.
  • Caregiving, including looking after siblings or relatives on a regular schedule — real responsibility, worth listing.
  • Informal gigs — babysitting, tutoring, lawn care, pet sitting — anything done consistently enough to describe with specifics.
  • Club or team leadership: treasurer, team captain, event organizer, student council, a school band or sports team.
  • Part-time or seasonal work, even if unrelated to the job you want — retail, food service, warehouse, camp counselor.
  • Certifications and short courses, from a community college class to a free online certificate.

You don't need all of these — two or three, described with real detail, beat a long list of vague bullets.

Step-by-step structure

With no work history to anchor the page, order matters more than usual. This sequence reads best to both a human and an ATS:

1. Contact information

Full name, phone number, email address, city and state (no full street address needed), and a LinkedIn URL if you have one. Use a professional-looking email, not a nickname from years ago.

2. Summary (not an objective)

A short summary works better than a traditional objective, because it tells the employer what you bring instead of only what you want. The trap is filling this space with filler that says nothing:

Weak: "Motivated self-starter eager to learn and grow in a fast-paced environment, looking for an opportunity to apply my skills."

Better: "Recent high school graduate with volunteer experience coordinating a weekly food pantry shift and part-time retail work handling customer transactions. Reliable, comfortable talking with the public, and looking for a full-time role in customer service."

The difference is specificity. The second version references something real, so it can't be copy-pasted onto anyone else's resume.

3. Education

With no work history, education moves up. List your school, graduation date, relevant coursework if it applies to the role, and any honors. Include GPA only if it's strong and recent (see the FAQ below).

4. Relevant experience / projects

This is where volunteering, projects, internships, and informal work go — grouped under "Experience," not hidden under "Extracurriculars." Treat each entry like a job: the role or project, the dates, and two or three bullets describing what you did.

5. Skills

List skills you can back up if asked: specific software, languages, certifications, or hands-on abilities (cash handling, first aid, a POS system, Excel). Avoid vague soft-skill words with nothing behind them — that's covered next.

6. Additional (optional)

Languages spoken, certifications that didn't fit above, or relevant hobbies only if they add something specific to the role.

US convention: skip a photo, your age, marital status, and "references available upon request." A photo can even work against you, since some employers set aside resumes with photos to avoid discrimination claims.

Common mistakes that cost interviews

1. Padding the page with irrelevant filler

Listing every video game or class you've taken to fill space signals that you're stretching, not that you have more to offer.

✅ Cut anything that doesn't support the role. A focused half-page beats a padded full page.

2. Inflating or inventing experience

Turning "helped at my aunt's restaurant sometimes" into "Assistant Manager" is the kind of exaggeration that unravels fast in an interview.

✅ Describe what you actually did, accurately. Honest and specific beats inflated and vague.

3. Using a two-column or heavily designed layout

A creative layout can look good and still fail an ATS. Many systems read left to right, top to bottom — a two-column layout can merge a job title from one column with a date from the other, or skip contact details in a text box.

✅ Use a single-column, text-based layout with standard section headings.

4. A generic, copy-pasted objective

"Seeking a position where I can utilize my skills and grow professionally" could go on any resume, for any job, from anyone. It tells the reader nothing.

✅ Replace it with a summary that references something specific you've actually done.

5. One resume sent to every job

A generic resume applied to a cashier role, a warehouse role, and an office role tends to match none of them well, since each posting uses different keywords for the same skills.

✅ Adjust the summary and skills section for each posting — it takes minutes and changes how well you match.

6. Listing soft skills with no evidence behind them

"Hardworking, team player, great communicator" as a standalone list is what everyone writes, and it's unverifiable on its own.

✅ Show the skill inside a bullet instead: a time you handled a difficult customer proves "communication" better than the word itself.

Example resume with no experience

The example below is illustrative and fictional — the name, school and details are invented to show the structure, not to be copied word for word.

Illustrative example; fictional data

Jordan Ellis

Aspiring Retail / Customer Service Associate · Springfield, IL · (555) 010-4482 · jordan.ellis@email.com
Summary

Recent high school graduate with volunteer experience coordinating a weekly food pantry shift and a seasonal retail job handling checkout and restocking. Reliable, comfortable with the public, and looking for a full-time customer service role.

Education

Springfield High School — Diploma, June 2026. Coursework included Business Communications and a semester-long consumer economics project.

Experience

Volunteer Coordinator (Weekly Shift) — Springfield Community Food Pantry · Sept 2025–Present

  • Managed check-in for about 40 visitors each Saturday shift, keeping the line moving.
  • Organized incoming donations by category and expiration date for other volunteers.
  • Trained two new volunteers on the check-in process during a staffing shortage.

Seasonal Sales Associate — Riverside Holiday Market · Nov 2025–Jan 2026

  • Operated a point-of-sale system and handled cash and card transactions at a seasonal retail booth.
  • Restocked merchandise and kept the display organized through four-hour shifts.
  • Answered customer questions about products and returns.
Skills

Point-of-sale (Square), cash handling, Microsoft Word and Excel (basic), bilingual English/Spanish (conversational).

This works because every bullet is concrete and checkable: a number of visitors, a system used, a task completed. Nothing here claims a title Jordan didn't hold or a result that can't be explained in an interview. The retail booth also gets framed around transferable tasks — POS systems, cash handling, customer questions — that matter for the target role.

Frequently asked questions

What do I put under experience if I've never had a job?

Don't leave the section blank and don't invent a job. List what you actually did: school or capstone projects, volunteering, internships, clubs or teams you led, and informal or seasonal work — described the same way you'd describe a paid role.

Should I include babysitting, family business work, or other informal jobs?

Yes, if it's real and you can describe it honestly. Babysitting shows responsibility, a family business shows you can follow a process and deal with people, and gigs like lawn care or tutoring show initiative. Frame them by what you handled, not just the title.

How long should a resume with no experience be?

One page. With no long work history to list, a single page is usually more than enough, and going longer tends to dilute what you do have rather than strengthen it.

Do I need an objective or a summary?

A short summary works better than a generic objective. Two or three sentences on what you're looking for and what you bring, grounded in something specific you've actually done, read as more credible than a line about being a fast learner.

Should I include my GPA?

Only if it's strong (generally 3.5 or higher) and you're still a student or graduated recently. Once you have relevant experience or you're a few years out of school, drop it — it adds little and can prompt questions you'd rather not field.
